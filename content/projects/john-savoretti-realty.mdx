## Context

John Savoretti Realty runs two offices on Long Island — Franklin Square in Nassau
County and Smithtown in Suffolk. Two markets, two sets of local knowledge, one
brand.

The interesting part isn't the office count. It's that a brokerage doesn't sell one
kind of thing. A house for sale, an apartment for rent and a commercial storefront
are all "listings," and on the surface they look like the same card in the same
grid. Underneath they aren't. A rental is priced per month. A sale has an asking
price and a status that moves through stages like pending. A commercial space is
described by its square footage, and "sold" may not be the word for what happens to
it.

Most small brokerage sites solve this by making one listing type with every field
optional, then hiding the empty ones. That works until someone enters a rental with
a sale status, and nothing stops them, and the card renders a lease with a **Sold**
badge on it.

## Constraints

- One person maintains this. Every design decision had to survive being handled by
  someone who is not looking at the code that day.
- Content changes had to stay out of the components. Prices, office hours and
  headline figures are the things that actually change; a site where changing an
  office phone number means editing JSX is a site that goes stale.
- Two offices had to feel like one business, not two microsites.

## Approach and key decisions

### One typed Listing model keyed on dealType

Listings come from a Postgres database fed by the MLS data, and every page reads
them through one typed `Listing` shape. `dealType` is `sale`, `rent` or `commercial`,
and it decides how a listing is priced (rentals show `/mo`), which route it lives
on and which filters apply.

It is a flat type, not a discriminated union. Fields that only some listings have,
like `beds`, `squareFeet` and `lotSize`, are optional, and `status` is one shared
set of values. That was the pragmatic call for feed data, where a row can arrive
with fields missing. The cost is the one I describe in the writing post: the type
can't stop a rental from carrying a sale-shaped field, so components guard on
what's present.

### One price formatter

`formatPrice` sits next to the `Listing` type and every card, hero and detail page
calls it, so a price reads the same everywhere and a rental always shows `/mo`.
Office details and headline figures live in typed data files rather than in the
components.

### Routes that match how people actually search

Rentals and commercial listings get dedicated routes rather than being filter states
on one index. Somebody looking for commercial space on Long Island is doing a
different search than somebody looking for a house, and a URL you can send to a
person — or that a search engine can index as a distinct page — is worth more than a
query parameter.

## Outcome

Three categories of listing run through one typed model. The two offices are one
site. Pricing and office details live in typed data, not in components. Deploys happen from a
git push through GitHub to Vercel, so publishing a change is a commit rather than an
event.

No traffic or conversion numbers here yet — the analytics haven't been in place long
enough for any figure to be honest, and a made-up one would be worse than none.

## What's next

The custom domain migration is in progress; the Vercel URL above is the live site
until it cuts over. Beyond that, the thing I'd add next is measurement: right now I
can describe what the site does structurally but not what it does commercially, and
those are different claims.

If I rebuilt it, I'd turn the flat `Listing` into a discriminated union on `dealType`,
with `status` per branch, so a rental can't carry a sale-only field or status. The
tradeoffs are in the writing post. After that, status transitions would be a state
machine rather than a set of values.
