## Context

A friend of mine is an independent barber in Smithtown, New York. His business runs
on Instagram: people find him there, they judge the work from photos, and then they
try to book. That last step is where a lot of independent operators lose people —
the link in bio goes somewhere generic, or somewhere slow, or somewhere that asks a
phone to render a desktop layout.

So the brief wasn't "a barbershop website." It was: a person is standing on a train
platform, they just tapped a link in an Instagram profile, and they have about four
seconds of patience. Get them to a booking.

## How the project was set up

I built it for a friend at no charge, scoped tightly, as a portfolio piece: proof
that I can design a whole site around one action. He decided not to pursue it, so
it was never launched on his own domain. The design reasoning below is the part
worth showing.

## Constraints

- Mobile is not the primary case, it is effectively the only case. Nearly everyone
  arrives from a phone, from Instagram, on cellular data.
- The scope was fixed up front and stayed fixed.
- The site had to be something Giancarlo could point at from a bio link without
  explanation. No onboarding, no "scroll down to find the button."

## Approach and key decisions

### Design the path, then the pages

I started from the single action that matters — book — and worked backwards. The
booking route is reachable from the first screen without scrolling, and it stays
reachable as you move down the page. Everything else on the site exists to answer
the questions someone asks *before* they commit: what does the work look like, where
is it, when is he open.

Nothing on the site competes with that one action. There's no newsletter, no
promotional interstitial, no second call-to-action fighting the first for attention.

### Treat speed as part of the design, not a follow-up task

On cellular, from a social app's in-app browser, a heavy page doesn't feel slow —
it feels broken, and people leave before they've formed an opinion about the
haircuts. Static rendering, images sized for the layout they land in, and no
client-side JavaScript doing work the server already did.

This is the argument I make to every service business: your site's speed *is* part
of your first impression, in the same way the shop being clean is.

### Instagram is the top of the funnel, not a competitor

The site links out to Instagram rather than trying to replicate a feed inside
itself. The photos live where he already posts them and where they're already
maintained. Embedding a feed would have meant a third-party script, a slower page,
and a second place for content to go stale.

## Outcome

Built and working on a preview link, mobile-first, doing the one job it was scoped to
do. It was never launched: my friend chose not to pursue the business online, so
there are no booking numbers to report, and this page won't invent any.

## What I took from it

Scope the build around one action and the rest of the decisions get easier. The next
client site starts from the same question: what is the single thing a visitor has to
be able to do in four seconds on a phone?
